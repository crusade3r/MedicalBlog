What is the contact greeting with the least transmission of pathogens, and how dangerous are contact greetings?

Introduction:

Many people get sick even though they maintain their health relatively well, keeping good hygeine and a healthy diet as common practice throughout their life. The secret to their supposed misfortune may actually lie in the way they greet people. In this short blog post, we will be going over how the three different contact greetings (Fist bump, Hand shake and a high five) transmit pathogens, and which one transmits the least pathogens. Hopefully, by the end of this post, you will be able to more effectively avoid illness, or atleast be able to derrive one of the many reasons on why you may getting sick out of the blue.

Before diving into these contact greetings, we should first take a look at how to identify a good and bad contact greeting. Pathogens (specifically bcteria and viruses), transmit through many different mediums, yet in this blog the only relevant method will be direct physical bridging between 2 parties. Assuming you, the reader/Person A, are a hygenic person, then for the rest of this blog the person transmitting pathogens will be the person you are greeting (Person B). To begin transmission, person B's hand must have some sort of contact with the pathogen first. With bacteria, this is almost guaranteed as bacteria on your hands can replicate rapidly and stay there for a very long time (asexual reproduction allows for prolonged living in a single habitat as long as the habitat has liveable conditions such as optimal living temperature). They also have 'biofilms' (Read more here) which protect them from threats like the immune system and antibiotics. With viruses, it becomes less likely. Person B must have been compromised and recently touched an exposed area where viral particles may be found (mucus), like the mouth and nose. They then must greet Person A with this mucus/heap of viral particles still on their hands. Viruses have no other method of being transmitted via contact due to their acellular existence (they cannot move on their own as they are not a cell, so non-lviing). Now to get sick from transmission, Person A must touch an exposed area themselves (eye/mouth/nose) with the same hand they greeted Person B with. The period of time in which transmission is likely from bacteria is significantly longer than viruses as bacteria have the ability to live on your hand for much longer (biofilms and abiotic reproduction - binary fission). This is one of the many reasons of why washing your hands is important.

The first greeting we will look at is the more promising one; the 'Hi' five/High five greeting (as of writing this I am unsure if there is an official name for a high five so i'll likely use both terms). A high five, in short, is a way to greet someone in a celebratory manner by essentially using someone else's hand to clap with you. In doing so, you make a loud noise which is associated with celebration and appraise. The greeting is not a relatively popular one, yet it seems to be on of the least transmitting methods of contact greetings in terms of pathogens (and, in turn, illness).

Of course, high fiving is essentially a 2-person clap, where time of contact between both parties lasts for a very short amount of time, so transmission would assumably be lower. Additionally, the surface area of contact is abount half of a handshake for example, being 100% involved in a handshake as to just 50% of your hand involved (entire hand to entire hand vs palm to palm). Pathogens have both a shorter window to cross between parties, but also a smaller area of pathogens are involved in transmission. These 2 factors greatly lead Hi fives to their well earned spot of the 2nd least transmitting contact greeting (of the three to be discussed).

A study conducted in Denmark carries out the implementation of the Hi Five greeting across multiple schools. After the Hi five intervention was placed, days where students were abscent due to illness and overall illness episodes reduced, showing the success of the Hi Five in terms of reducing transmission.

The next transmission greeting is a more simple one; the fist bump. A fist bump is a simple quick tap between Person A and B's knuckles and some contact of the outer side of the fingers. The fist bump is a short but globaly popular message/symbol, and still holds relevance in terms of contact greeting. Gladly, it is one of the least transmitting contact greetings and the No.1 least in terms of the 3 being discussed here. It holds the Gold award for both the least surface area involved between parties, and the relatively shortest contact time between parties (note that contact time varies widely between people in regards to high fives and fist bumps, but the general consensus is that fist bumps are faster. This makes it difficult for bacteria to transfer and even more difficult for viruses, and in turn, makes it a lot easier for Person A to avoid illness.

On the other hand (no pun intended), handshakes are significantly worse contact greetings compared to both hi fives and fist bumps. Unfortunately, handshakes seem to be the most popular greeting method globally. A handshake is a gesture associated with respect and common ground, but also with order and business. It involves Person A and B to interlock their hands at the intersections of their thumbs and firmly grip one anothers hands (I am convinced that it was an evil doctor who decided that the tighter the grip of a handshake, the greater the respect??). If you have made it this far into the blog, you must be laughing to yourself by now (as I am writing this), realising that youve been doing this greeting all your life and only now noticing just how amazing an oportunity this is for pathogens to spread. Firstly, as you know, handshakes have by far the worst surface area of contact (entire hand) and the worst contact time between parties (especially 'business' handshakes). However, a second factor is now just how easy it is to actually cross the bridge between Person A and B. Handshakes offer viruses an amazing opportunity to be mechanically forced onto Person A's hand, as they last for so long, and are so tightly compressed. Additionally, as they are so tightly compressed, it reduces the risk of the virus simply being blown away by a greater force (e.g. wind). Even with a 'sloppy'/impropper handshake, this risk is greatly nullified compared to the other 2 contact greatings. It seriously is astounding just how dangerous a handshake can be, and I am not alone in that conclusion: This study used a sterile glove imersed in a film of nonpathogenic Ecoli (hopefully not the equivalent of who we are shaking hands with), and then preformed a handshake and a fist bump between the now compromised glove and a differnet sterile glove. Shockingly, the handshake glove recieved twice as many Ecoli compared to the fist bump. As you can see, handshakes are terrible really.

Now while knowing how to greet your friends can be usefull, just how usefull really is it? Is there really any significance at all between a handshake and a fist bump? "I shake the hands of my friends all the time and im not sick?!". Well, there are an absurd amount of factors contributing to the your wellbeing. A contact greeting can be considered the first possible step to an illness, as they must first reach the vessel they 'wish' to infect and then enter the vessel via many different methods. As pathogens can take many different routes to make you ill, there is no 1 defined way of preventing illness via pathogen. However, i still believe it is well worth noting as many illnesses arive simply due to shaking the hand of the wrong person for slightly too long. If there were lessons to take from this blog, it is to avoid contact greetings from ill people as they likely do not consider wether or not there is mucus on their hands or not, if you are to perform a handshake - which you shouldn't - then please do not hold firmly and for too long, and finally dont touch your own face after greeting someone without washing your hands.

To conclude, I am no germaphobe, far from it. I simply don't see the point in getting sick over something so simply avoidable and potentially risking your own life. The purpose of the blog is both to bring more light to how we greet each other and the danger we are putting each other under, and also to signify the importance of hand washing hygiene. I'm not saying you need to scrub your hands after accidentally itching your nose, but you should be more careful of where on your face your making contact with while having unwashed hands, especially when you your self are sick.
